Introduction

Starting a doctoral program in rhetoric and composition means entering a field with its own vocabulary, culture, and unspoken expectations — and doing so largely without a map. This guide is an attempt to provide that map. It was designed for students at every stage of their PhD journey. Inside, you will find year-by-year planning templates, explanations of major milestones like comprehensive exams and the prospectus, career-track guides for six different professional paths in the field, and a glossary that defines the acronyms and terms that tend to get used without explanation.

Nothing in this document is a substitute for conversations with your advisor — requirements vary by program, and your advisor is always your best source for specifics. Think of this guide as a starting point for those conversations, a reference you can return to throughout your five years, and a reminder that the path to a PhD is rarely linear but is absolutely navigable.

These appendices are designed to help graduate students plan Years 1–5 in the program. Use them as guides, not strict rules. Consult your advisor and program handbook to adapt timelines and deadlines to your institution.

What's inside

Milestones & Requirements — every required and suggested item across Years 1–8+, filterable by your career track (tenure-track, teaching/WPA, writing center, industry, technical & professional communication, or community-engaged public rhetorics). Every field is editable, and you can delete any item that doesn't apply to you.

Semester Planner — a term-by-term grid for courses and other responsibilities, Years 1–5, fully editable and expandable.

Individual Development Plan (IDP) — yearly goals across degree progress, skills, career, personal wellbeing, and funding, plus a summary table that fills in automatically as you go.

Glossary — key acronyms and terms used throughout (RCWS, WPA, IDP, prospectus, etc.), plus room to add your own.

Committee Selection Summary

Aim for a committee with (1) subject expertise, (2) methodological expertise, (3) intellectual alignment, and (4) an external reader when appropriate.

Important note: If you don't mesh with the faculty member at your university who is supposed to be an expert in your subject matter, don't work with them. Identify another faculty member willing to support your research and find other ways to develop your knowledge of the research subject through other mentoring relationships.
